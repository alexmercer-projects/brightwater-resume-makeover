02 · What changed

Eight fixes, and why each one matters.

None of this is decoration. Each change makes the page easier for a recruiter to skim or for hiring software to read.

  1. 1

    Photo and two columns → became One column, no photo

    Two-column layouts and text boxes can come out of an applicant tracking system in the wrong order, and in the US most recruiters would rather not see a photo. One column reads cleanly everywhere.

  2. 2

    Contact details in a sidebar with icons → became Plain contact line under the name

    Icons are images, so a parser may skip them, and sidebars get separated from the name. Plain text right under the name never goes missing.

  3. 3

    A generic objective → became A three-line summary aimed at the role

    An objective says what the candidate wants. A summary says what they bring, using the words from the posting they're applying to.

  4. 4

    “My Journey” → became “Experience”

    Creative headings are charming and easy for software to misfile. Standard headings (Summary, Experience, Skills, Education) land in the right fields.

  5. 5

    Duties → became Results with numbers

    Every bullet now starts with a verb and ends with an outcome. The numbers here are example data. On a real resume they come from the person, and I ask rather than guess.

  6. 6

    Three different date styles → became One date format, aligned right

    Mixed formats like “2021-now” and “June '18” look careless and can confuse date parsing. Month and year, the same way every time.

  7. 7

    Skill bars → became Grouped skills that match the posting

    A bar that's four-fifths full doesn't tell a recruiter anything, and software can't read it. Named tools and methods can be searched.

  8. 8

    Hobbies and “references available” → became Certifications

    References are assumed, and hobbies rarely earn the space. That room goes to certifications the posting actually asks about.

03 · Bullet rewrites

From duties to results.

The pattern: start with a verb, say what changed, show the number. In a real job I ask for the figures. I never make them up.

07 · How I’d do it

The process, start to finish.

A finished job comes back as a PDF and an editable Word file, plus the LinkedIn headline and About text ready to paste. Both files for this demo are above: PDF and Word.

  1. 01

    Read the target job

    I start from one or two real postings the person wants, not from the old resume.

  2. 02

    Pull the keywords

    Tools, methods and titles that appear in the posting go on a list, so the rewrite uses the same words.

  3. 03

    Ask for the real numbers

    Team sizes, volumes, before-and-after figures. If a number isn't known, the bullet says what changed without one.

  4. 04

    Rebuild the layout

    One column, standard headings, real text (no images of text), and a font that survives any computer.

  5. 05

    Rewrite every bullet

    Verb first, result last, one or two lines each. Duties that don't support the target job get cut.

  6. 06

    Check it

    Paste-to-plain-text test, PDF and Word exports, a spell check, and the same edits carried into LinkedIn.
